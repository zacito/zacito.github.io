AI 101

A plain-language introduction to artificial intelligence: what it is, what it does, and how the chatbots actually work.

What is AI?

Artificial intelligence is software that learns patterns from examples instead of only following hand-written rules. Traditional software does exactly what a programmer told it to do. AI is trained: you show it enormous amounts of data, and it works out the patterns itself.

Most of today's AI is built on neural networks — programs loosely modeled on how brains connect — trained on data at a massive scale. When people say "AI" now, they usually mean generative AI: models that create new text, images, audio, and code instead of just sorting or labeling things.

The chatbots — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok — are large language models, or LLMs. An LLM is trained on huge amounts of text to do one thing: predict what comes next. At enough scale, that single skill turns into answering questions, writing, translating, coding, and holding a conversation.

What does it do?

Things people use AI for every day:

  • Answer and explain. Questions on any topic, explained at whatever level you ask for.
  • Write and edit. Emails, documents, articles, summaries, and rewrites in a different tone.
  • Code. Write, explain, and debug computer programs.
  • See and hear. Describe and analyze images, transcribe speech, and read text aloud.
  • Create media. Generate images, music, and increasingly video from a written description.
  • Work with your files. Search your documents and answer questions from them instead of from memory.
  • Act as an agent. Use tools, browse the web, and carry out multi-step tasks, not just chat about them.

Just as important is what it doesn't do. An LLM doesn't know things the way a person does, and it can be confidently wrong — making up facts is called a hallucination. It knows nothing newer than its training, and it has no goals or awareness of its own. The right mental model is a fast, extremely well-read assistant whose work you still check.

How does it work, in 30 seconds?

During training, the model reads trillions of words and keeps adjusting billions of internal dials — its parameters — until its guesses about the next word get very good. Text is chopped into chunks called tokens, and the model works one token at a time. Inside, a mechanism called attention, from the Transformer architecture, lets it weigh which earlier words matter most to the word it's producing. Everything else — the chat, the reasoning, the tools — is built on top of that.
